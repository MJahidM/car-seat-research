1Where it stands

The short version, if nothing else gets read.

The middle seat is out for a rear-facing seat. Not because of width, and not because it lacks lower anchors. Because the centre seatback contains a fold-down armrest that is not structural, and a rear-facing seat leans directly against it.

The seat goes on a window position, rear-facing, installed with the lower anchors. Passenger side, so he loads from the curb rather than the traffic side.

It is not an unsafe seat and it installs beautifully. It is simply not the safest, which was the stated criterion.

2The car: what the CX-70 will and will not do

Everything below is from the Mazda owner's manual for the plug-in hybrid, confirmed against a physical inspection of a CX-70 by Cars.com.

Lower anchors

Two sets only, at the two window positions. None in the centre. The nearest pair to the middle are the inboard anchors of the two window seats, and those sit 500 mm apart, which is 19.7 inches. Standard spacing that car seats are built for is about 11 inches.

Mazda permits using that far-apart pair only if the car seat's own manual specifically says it may be installed on anchors 19.7 inches apart. Essentially no car seat sold in the United States says that.

"Child-restraint systems with rigid ISOFIX/LATCH attachments cannot be installed on the center seating position."

This is why the PIPA urbn's pull-out rigid arms have nothing to grab in the middle.

Support leg, also called a load leg

"It is not able to set the child-restraint system with the support leg in the rear center seat."

This appears in the block of the manual that applies to United States vehicles. Any seat with a floor-bracing pole is disqualified from the centre. None of the seats under consideration has one.

Seat belt installation in the centre: permitted

Mazda's own suitability table rates the rear centre "Yes (U)", meaning suitable for universal-category child restraints secured by the vehicle belt. The manual includes a procedure for it, "How to Use the Automatic Locking Mode": pull the belt all the way out, then let it feed back, and the retractor switches to a mode where it tightens but will not loosen.

A belt installation is exactly as safe as a lower-anchor installation. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration and the American Academy of Pediatrics both say so. Lower anchors were introduced to make installing easier, not safer.

Top tether anchors

Three of them, one behind each seating position including the middle, on the back of the second-row seatback, reached from the cargo area. They may sit under a flap of carpet. Confirmed in Mazda's anchor diagram and by physical inspection.

The armrest, which is the decisive problem

The centre seatback contains a fold-down armrest. Mazda's manual says of it:

"be careful not to apply excessive force to the armrest such as by sitting on it, otherwise, it could be deformed or damaged"

That is the manufacturer stating the panel is not structural. A rear-facing seat leans back against exactly that panel. No amount of tightening fixes what the seat is bearing against. This is the reason the centre is off the table, and it came from the technician, not from the spec sheets.

The 11 inch figure is the gap between hardware, not the width of the seating position. A car seat base does not have to fit between the buckles; it has to sit flat and cinch tight. Bases bridge over low buckles routinely. This measurement alone does not rule out the centre. The armrest does.

  • Bulky, heavy, not safer
  • Brand new category, no long field history
  • You have to actually use the rotation for it to be worth anything, and parents find it annoying and stop
  • The rain problem: every rotation means re-tightening, and on a bad-weather day you skip it and drive with a loose seat
  • If one is unavoidable, Graco, not Evenflo

The centre seat

The armrest can come down onto a rear-facing seat in a crash. Mazda's own manual supports this; see section 2.

Lower anchors versus seat belt

  • Never use both at once. Exception: Nuna and one other brand permit it.
  • Neither is safer. The system is simply not designed for both.
  • Lower anchors are rated to roughly 30 to 40 pounds, depending on the seat.
  • He likes lower anchors rear-facing. Forward-facing later, he prefers the seat belt.

On the Extend2Fit specifically

  • It is a 3-in-1: rear-facing, then forward-facing, then booster.
  • He does not recommend using its booster mode. Buy a dedicated booster when that time comes.

Three things determine real-world safety

  1. Crash performance. How much force reaches the child.
  2. How long the child stays rear-facing. The single biggest lever at an infant's age, set by the rear-facing weight and height ceiling.
  3. Whether the seat gets installed correctly. Roughly half of all seats are installed wrong, so a seat that is hard to get right is less safe in practice than its lab numbers suggest.

The Cybex Callisto G 360, fairly stated

Genuinely excellent at: installation, scoring 9.5 on the belt and 9.0 on lower anchors, near the top of everything tested. It has a real anti-rebound bar in the base, linear side-impact protection, a steel frame, and the rotation saves your back twice a day. It passes every federal standard.

Falls short at: crash score 6.6, the lowest in the group, with the head reading just below average and the chest sensor reading significantly above average. Rear-facing stops at 40 lb and 43.3 in, roughly a year less rear-facing time than the alternatives. 35 lb of seat and 19.5 to 20 in wide, the heaviest and among the widest.

The argument hiding in the preference for it is real: a seat that actually gets tightened properly every time beats a theoretically better seat installed sloppily. The Extend2Fit's belt installation score of 6.4 is genuinely mediocre. That said, a technician with 30,000 installations predicts the rotation stops getting used.

Pediatricians are experts in children's health, not crash engineering. What spreads through a group of colleagues is usually what is pleasant to live with and well regarded. The Cybex earns that honestly. It is just not the safest.

7The December 2026 federal standard

FMVSS 213a. FMVSS stands for Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard. 213a is the new side-impact test, effective 5 December 2026.

It applies by date of manufacture, not date of purchase. Any seat built before 5 December 2026 remains legal to sell and to use, and retailers will keep selling older stock into 2027. The reliable check is the date-of-manufacture sticker on the bottom of the seat, not the marketing copy.

What the two Extend2Fit versions actually claim

VersionPriceWhat the manufacturer's own page says
Extend2Fit 3-in-1$269.99"Side-impact tested according to FMVSS 213a with the built-in 5-point harness system"
Extend2Fit 3-in-1 featuring Anti-Rebound Bar$339.99"Side-impact tested for occupant retention" — no mention of 213a anywhere on the page

"Side-impact tested for occupant retention" is in-house test language, not the federal standard. So within one product family, the cheaper version names the newer standard and the more expensive one does not. This reverses the earlier advice that the $100 anti-rebound bar upgrade was worthwhile.

Honest caveat: a missing claim is not proof of non-compliance, and it could be stale web copy. But when two sibling products sit side by side and only one names the standard, that is a signal.

Compliance with federal car seat standards is self-certified. There is no third-party verification and no official list of certified models.

Planned for later

  • The switch from lower anchors to the seat belt. Lower anchors are rated to roughly 30 to 40 lb. The seats above rear-face to 50 lb. the child will outgrow the anchors before he outgrows rear-facing, so there will be a re-installation using the belt. The exact number is on the sticker on the seat.
  • A dedicated booster in a few years. Do not rely on an all-in-one's booster mode.
  • The top tether gets used from the day the child turns forward-facing, which should be as late as possible, ideally past his fourth birthday.

Install check, whichever seat is chosen

  • Buckle the belt or connect the anchors first, then tighten.
  • Put body weight on the base while pulling tight. A hand is not enough.
  • Grab the seat at the belt path and pull hard side to side and front to back.
  • Under one inch of movement passes. More than that fails.
  • Rear-facing, the harness straps come out of the shell at or just below his shoulders, and the chest clip sits level with his armpits.
